# The Water Cycle

Look at a puddle after the rain. A few hours later it is gone. Where did the water go? The answer is a story that has been repeating for millions of years, and it connects the oceans, the clouds, the rivers and even the plants around you. In this chapter we follow a single drop of water on its journey.

## Water on Our Planet

About 71 percent of the Earth's surface is covered with water. Nearly 97 percent of all that water is in the oceans, and it is salty. Only about 3 percent is fresh water, and most of that fresh water is locked up as ice in glaciers and ice caps. The water in rivers and lakes that we use every day is a very small fraction of the total.

Because fresh water is limited, it is important to understand how nature keeps renewing it. The process that renews it is called the water cycle.

## Evaporation

When the Sun heats water in oceans, lakes and rivers, some of the liquid water changes into an invisible gas called water vapour. This change from liquid to gas is called evaporation. Evaporation happens faster when it is warm, when it is windy and when the surface of the water is wide.

Plants also add water vapour to the air. They take in water through their roots and release a part of it through tiny openings in their leaves. This process is called transpiration. Together, evaporation and transpiration put large amounts of water vapour into the atmosphere.

## Condensation and Clouds

Warm air carrying water vapour rises. As it rises it becomes cooler. Cool air cannot hold as much water vapour, so the vapour changes back into tiny droplets of liquid water. This change from gas to liquid is called condensation. Billions of these tiny droplets gather around specks of dust in the air and form clouds.

You can see condensation at home. When you take a cold glass of water out of the refrigerator, small drops soon form on the outside of the glass. These drops come from water vapour in the air that touched the cold surface.

## Precipitation

Inside a cloud, tiny droplets join together and grow bigger and heavier. When they become too heavy to stay in the air, they fall to the ground. Water that falls from clouds is called precipitation. It can fall as rain, snow, sleet or hail, depending on the temperature of the air.

## Collection and Runoff

After precipitation, some water flows over the ground and collects in streams, rivers, lakes and finally the oceans. This flow is called runoff. Some water soaks into the soil and moves slowly underground. Water stored underground is called groundwater, and people reach it by digging wells and tube wells.

Snow that falls on high mountains may stay frozen for a long time. When the weather becomes warmer the snow melts, and the melted water feeds rivers that carry it downhill.

## Why the Water Cycle Matters

The water cycle has no beginning and no end. The Sun provides the energy that drives it. It supplies fresh water to plants, animals and people, and it helps spread heat around the planet. If we pollute rivers or waste water, we make it harder for the cycle to give us clean water.
